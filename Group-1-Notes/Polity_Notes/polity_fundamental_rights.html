UPSC / STATE PSC INDIAN POLITY HIGH-YIELD REVISION NOTES

Fundamental Rights (Part III, Articles 12–35)

The Magna Carta of India • Judicial Review • 6 Categories of Rights • Writs & Exceptions
1. Overview & General Principles

Philosophical Foundations

  • Source Origin: Borrowed from the US Bill of Rights.
  • Historical Identity: Termed the "Magna Carta of India".
  • Core Objective: Establish a "Government of Laws and not of Men"; preserve political democracy and individual liberty against State encroachment.
  • Justiciability: Directly enforceable by the courts (Art 32 via Supreme Court, Art 226 via High Courts).

Limits, Amendments & Emergency

  • Not Absolute: Subject to reasonable restrictions (public order, morality, sovereignty, state security).
  • Amendability: Amendable by Parliament via Art 368 without altering the Basic Structure (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).
  • Emergency Immunity (Art 352):
    • Articles 20 & 21 can NEVER be suspended (44th Amendment Act, 1978).
    • Art 19 is automatically suspended only during war or external aggression (Art 358), NOT armed rebellion.
2. Preliminary Provisions (Articles 12 & 13)

Article 12: Definition of "State"

Authorities against whom Fundamental Rights are primarily enforceable:

  • Union: Govt & Parliament of India (Executive + Legislature).
  • States: Govt & Legislature of each State.
  • Local Authorities: Municipalities, Panchayats, District Boards, Improvement Trusts.
  • Other Authorities: Statutory bodies & instrumentalities (LIC, ONGC, SAIL).
  • Judiciary: Excluded when performing judicial functions; included when performing administrative tasks (Naresh Mirajkar case).
  • Bodies NOT Falling Under "State" (Article 12 Exclusions):
    ▫ BCCI: Zeev Telefilms v. Union of India (2005) — Held that BCCI is a private autonomous society, not financially, functionally, or administratively dominated by the Government, hence NOT "State" under Art 12 (though amenability to public duty writs under Art 226 remains open).
    ▫ NCERT: Chander Mohan Khanna v. NCERT (1991) — Supreme Court held that NCERT is largely an autonomous registered society engaged in research and training without sovereign or governmental monopoly character, hence NOT an instrumentality of the State.

Article 13: Shield of FRs & Judicial Review

  • 13(1) Pre-Constitutional Laws: If these violates fundamental rights are of no values (Doctrine of Severability & Doctrine of Eclipse).
    • ▫ Pre-Constitutional Example (IPC, 1860): Section 377 IPC (enacted under British rule in 1860) was challenged under Art 13(1) for violating Arts 14, 15, 19, and 21 in Naz Foundation v. Govt. of NCT of Delhi (Delhi HC, 2009), reversed in Suresh Koushal (2013), and finally read down unanimously by the Supreme Court Constitution Bench in Navtej Singh Johar v. Union of India (2018) to decriminalize consensual same-sex relations among adults applying the Doctrine of Severability.
  • 13(2) Post-Constitutional Laws: State shall not make laws taking away FRs; void ab initio.
  • 13(3) Definition of "Law": Includes Ordinances, orders, bye-laws, rules, regulations, notifications, customs having force of law.
  • 13(4) Constitutional Amendments: Added by 24th Amendment (1971) — amends are not "laws" under Art 13. Upheld in Kesavananda, subject to Basic Structure.
3. Category I: Right to Equality (Articles 14–18)

Article 14

  • Art 14: Equality Before Law & Equal Protection of the Laws:
    • 14(a) Equality Before Law
    ▫ Origin: British constitutional tradition; core element of A.V. Dicey's Rule of Law (expounded in The Law of the Constitution).
    ▫ Dicey's 3 Core Postulates:
      1. No one is above law:
      2. Equality Before the Law:
      3. Rights of the citizens are decided by court
    ▫ ★ Exam Distinction (India vs. UK):
      • Postulates (1) and (2) apply directly to the Indian constitutional framework.
      • Postulate (3) does NOT apply to India: in india rights of citizens is decide by indian constitution because we have written constitution.
    • 14(b) Equal Protection of the Laws
    ▫ Origin: Adopted from USA.
    ▫ Core Principle: Equality between equal people and equal circumstances.
    ▫ Protects Natural Justice: A retired principal and a fresher applying for a clerk post cannot be treated as identical unequals.
    ▫ Exceptions: In India, the President, Governors, and foreign ambassadors enjoy special constitutional privileges (immunities).

Article 15

  • Art 15: No Discrimination on 5 Grounds: Religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth (only).
    • 15(1)[State]&15(2)[Citizen]: Equal access to public places, wells, shops.
    • 15(3): Special provisions for women & children.
    • 15(4): SC, ST advancement in education.
    ▫ 1951: 15(4) was added via 1st constitutional Ammendment , based on Educational backwardness and untouchability
    ▫ Religious Conversion Example (AP / Telangana): This happened as there is no untouchability exists in Christianity, said by the court.
    • Under the Constitution (Scheduled Castes) Order, 1950 (Para 3), SC status is restricted strictly to Hindu, Sikh, and Buddhist persons.
    • When persons from SC convert to Christianity, they lose their SC status, state governments classify them as BC-C (Backward Class Group-C) and issue BC-C community certificate

    • 15(5): OBC Reservation in higher education
    ▫ 2005: Enacted via 93rd Constitutional Amendment Act.
    ▫ 2006: Enacted the Central Educational Institutions (Reservation in Admission) Act (27% OBC quota).
    ▫ 2007–08: Challenged and upheld by the Supreme Court in Ashoka Kumar Thakur v. Union of India (upheld 15(5) & OBC quota is not against equality).

    • 15(6): 10% EWS quota (103rd Amendment Act, 2019).
    📌 Basis of resesvation: Neither caste nor financial backwardness:
    • Target Group: Exclusively for citizens who are NOT covered under SC, ST, or OBC/SEBC quotas (General Category poor).
    • Basis: Purely economic weakness (income/assets), completely divorced from caste/social backwardness.
    • Institutional Exception: Applies to all educational institutions (private aided & unaided), but does NOT apply to Minority Educational Institutions covered under Article 30(1).

Article 16

  • Art 16: Equal Employment Opportunities:
    • 16(1): Government is not allow to show discrimination in government employments
    • 16(2): Defined discrimination on 7 grounds Religion, race, caste, sex, place of birth, descent & residence.
    • 16(3): Any state government, local government or union territory govenrment can decide local general reservartions
    • 16(4): Initial Entry-Level Quotas for Backward Classes in government employment
    ▫ Core Mandate: Enables the State to provide reservations in appointments for any "backward class of citizens" which, in the opinion of the State, is not adequately represented in public services.
    ▫ 1992 Landmark Verdict: In Indra Sawhney v. Union of India (Mandal Case), a 9-judge Constitution Bench held:
      • 50% Vertical Cap: Total reservations cannot exceed 50% in a given year (except in far-flung/extraordinary areas).
      • Exclusion of Creamy Layer: Applies strictly to OBCs/SEBCs.
      • Bar on Promotions: Article 16(4) applies only at the entry stage of initial recruitment, explicitly outlawing reservations in promotions.
    • 16(4A): Reservations in Promotions for SCs & STs:
    ▫ 1995 (Legislative Counter): To nullify the promotional ban from Indra Sawhney, Parliament enacted the 77th Constitutional Amendment Act, 1995, inserting Article 16(4A) to permit reservation in promotions for SCs and STs if inadequately represented.
    ▫ 2001 (Consequential Seniority): Strengthened by the 85th Constitutional Amendment Act, 2001, conferring retrospective consequential seniority to SC/ST officers promoted through reservation quotas.
    ▫ Constitutional Scrutiny:
      • M. Nagaraj v. Union of India (2006): Upheld 16(4A) validity, but placed 3 preconditions on the State: prove (i) backwardness, (ii) inadequacy of representation, and (iii) maintenance of administrative efficiency (under Art 335).
      • Jarnail Singh v. Lachhmi Narain Gupta (2018): Modified M. Nagaraj — ruled that the State need not collect quantifiable data on backwardness for SCs/STs (presumed backward), but introduced the Creamy Layer exclusion for SC/ST promotions.
    • 16(4B): 50% rule not applicable for backward posts
    ▫ Enacted: 81st Constitutional Amendment Act, 2000 (overruling the carry-forward limitation imposed in Indra Sawhney).
    ▫ Operational Mechanism: Unfilled reserved vacancies of an earlier recruitment year are treated as a distinct, independent class of "backlog vacancies".
    ▫ Ceiling Immunity: When filled in subsequent recruitment years, backlog vacancies shall NOT be combined with the regular vacancies of that year to determine the 50% ceiling (can exceed 50% for that drive).
    📌 APPSC Prelims Catch:
    • Art 16(4) applies to all backward classes (OBCs, SCs, STs) for initial recruitment.
    • Art 16(4A) applies strictly to SCs and STs only (OBCs have NO promotional reservation).
    • Art 16(4B) is the constitutional shield that prevents backlog recruitment drives from being struck down under the 50% quota rule.
    • 16(5): Religion-Specific Office Exception:
    ▫ Constitutional Shield: Permits laws requiring that the holder of an office connected with a religious or denominational institution (or member of its governing body) must profess that particular religion or denomination.
    ▫ Exemption from 16(2): Operates as a legitimate exception to the rule barring religious discrimination in public/state-regulated appointments (e.g., Hindu Religious Endowments/TTD board posts, Waqf Boards).

    • 16(6): 10% EWS quota in government jobs (103rd Amendment Act, 2019).

Article 18: Abolition of Titles

  • Art 18: Abolition of Titles:
    •     Article 18(1): The State cannot give titles to anyone. Only military and academic ranks are allowed.
    • Article 18(2): No citizen of India shall accept any title from any foreign State. (Absolute ban; no provision for Presidential consent).
    • Article 18(3): A foreigner working for the Indian government cannot accept a foreign title without the President's permission.
    • Article 18(4): Prohibits any person (citizen or foreigner) working for the Indian government from taking gifts, pay, or jobs from a foreign country without the President's permission.

    • Judicial Precedent — Balaji Raghavan v. UOI (1996): National Awards (Bharat Ratna, Padma Vibhushan, Padma Bhushan, Padma Shri) are civilian decorations, not titles within the meaning of Article 18(1). However, they cannot be used as prefixes or suffixes to the awardee's name.

Article 20: Protection Against Conviction

  • 20(1) No Ex-Post Facto Law: No retroactive criminal conviction or enhanced penalty (applies to criminal law only, not civil/tax).
    ▫ Can take forcible finger prints
    ▫ Can't force people 
  • 20(2) No Double Jeopardy: Cannot be prosecuted & punished twice for the same offence (applies only before judicial courts/tribunals).
  • 20(3) No Self-Incrimination: Accused cannot be compelled to witness against self.
    • Selvi Case: Involuntary narco-analysis, polygraph, brain mapping violate Art 20(3).

    • Added by 86th Amendment Act, 2002.

Article 22: Protection Against Arrest & Detention

  • Part I: Punitive Detention (After a Crime is Committed):
    ▫ 22(1) Two Core Rights:
      • Right to know grounds: Police must inform the arrested person why they are arrested as soon as possible.
      • Right to a lawyer: Right to consult and be defended by a legal practitioner of one's choice.
    ▫ 22(2) 24-Hour Production Rule: Must produce the arrested person before the nearest Magistrate within 24 hours (excluding travel time).
    ▫ 22(2) Continued Detention: Cannot keep the person in police custody beyond 24 hours without the Magistrate's permission.
    ▫ 22(3) Exceptions to Part I: The rights in 22(1) and 22(2) do NOT apply to:
      • An enemy alien (citizen of a country at war with India).
      • A person arrested under any preventive detention law.
  • Part II: Preventive Detention (Before a Crime is Committed):
    ▫ Nature of Detention: Precautionary action to prevent future harm; based on suspicion, not a formal criminal charge or trial.
    ▫ 22(4) Maximum 3-Month Limit: A person cannot be held longer than 3 months unless an Advisory Board (judges/qualified to be High Court judges) reviews and approves the extension.
      • Note: The 44th Amendment Act (1978) reduced this to 2 months, but that reduction has never been officially notified/implemented by the government.
    ▫ 22(5) Informing Grounds & Representation:
      • The authority must communicate the grounds of detention to the person as soon as possible.
      • Must give the earliest opportunity to make a representation against the detention order.
    ▫ 22(6) Public Interest Exception: The authority can withhold facts if disclosing them is against public interest.
    ▫ 22(7) Parliament's Law-Making Powers: Parliament alone has power to prescribe:
      • Circumstances/classes of cases where detention can exceed 3 months without an Advisory Board opinion.
      • The maximum period of detention under any preventive detention law.
      • Procedure to be followed by the Advisory Board.
  • Key Judicial Precedents & Distinctions:
    ▫ D.K. Basu v. State of West Bengal (1997): Supreme Court laid down mandatory arrest guidelines (arrest memo, informing relatives, medical checkup every 48 hours).
    ▫ Civil vs Criminal Arrest: Protections under 22(1) and 22(2) apply to criminal arrests by police, not to civil arrests, tax recovery, or deportation of foreigners.

5. Exploitation, Religion & Minority Rights (Arts 23–30)

Exploitation (23–24)

  • Art 23: Bans human trafficking and Boanded Labour System(Begar, unpaid forced labour).
    • Available vs State & private persons.
    • Exception: Compulsory state service for public purpose (no bias on religion/caste).
  • Art 24: Bans child labour under 14 years in factories, mines, hazardous work.
    • Child & Adolescence Labour Prohibition Act (2016): Complete ban <14 yrs; 14–18 yrs banned in hazardous jobs.

Religious Freedom (25–28)

  • Art 25: Conscience, practice, profess, propagate (no right to forcibly convert). Kirpans allowed.
    • Subject to: Public order, morality, health.
  • Art 26: Denominational rights to manage religious affairs & property.
  • Art 27: No taxes for promoting any religion (Fee permitted, Tax banned).
  • Art 28: Religious instruction:
    • State-funded: Prohibited.
    • State-aided/recognized: Voluntary.
    • Endowments/Trusts: Permitted.

Minority Rights (29–30)

  • Art 29: Cultural Protection:
    • 29(1): Right to conserve language, script, culture (all citizen groups).
    • 29(2): No denial of admission to state-aided schools on religion, race, caste, language.
  • Art 30: Educational Setup:
    • Exclusively for Religious & Linguistic Minorities (not caste/race!).
    • 30(1): Right to establish & run institutions of choice.
    • 30(1A): Fair compensation on property acquisition.
    • 30(2): Non-discrimination in state aid.
6. Constitutional Remedies (Art 32) & The 5 Writs

Dr. B.R. Ambedkar: "Heart and Soul of the Constitution". Article 32 is itself a Fundamental Right!
➔ Art 32 vs Art 226: SC jurisdiction under Art 32 is narrow (FRs only) but mandatory; HC jurisdiction under Art 226 is broader (FRs + ordinary legal rights) but discretionary.

Writ Literal Meaning Purpose & Target Key Limitations
Habeas Corpus "To have the body of" Releases persons illegally detained. Enforceable against BOTH public & private entities. Cannot be issued if detention is lawful or authorized by court.
Mandamus "We Command" Commands public officials/bodies to perform an official statutory duty. Cannot be issued vs President, Governors, or private individuals.
Prohibition "To forbid" Issued by higher court to lower court/tribunal to prevent exceeding jurisdiction (Preventive). Available only against judicial & quasi-judicial authorities.
Certiorari "To be certified" Quashes order already passed by lower court/tribunal without jurisdiction or in legal error (Curative + Preventive). Available vs judicial, quasi-judicial, and administrative bodies (post-1991).
Quo-Warranto "By what authority" Inquires into legality of claim to a substantive public office; prevents illegal usurpation. Cannot be issued for ministerial or private offices. Can be filed by non-aggrieved.
7. Operational Exceptions (Articles 31A–35)

Saving of Certain Laws (31A, 31B, 31C)

  • Art 31A: Saves agrarian reform laws, acquisition of estates, amalgamation of corporations from challenge under Arts 14 and 19.
  • Art 31B & Ninth Schedule: Shields specified Acts from FR violations.
    • I.R. Coelho (2007): Laws put in 9th Schedule after April 24, 1973 (Kesavananda date) are subject to Judicial Review if they violate Basic Structure.
  • Art 31C: Saves laws implementing Directive Principles under Art 39(b) & 39(c) from Arts 14 & 19. "Where Art 31C comes in, Art 14 goes out."

Armed Forces, Martial Law & Legislation (33–35)

  • Art 33: Empowers Parliament alone to restrict/abrogate FRs of Armed Forces, Police, Intelligence personnel for discipline.
  • Art 34: Restricts FRs during Martial Law in any area; empowers Parliament to indemnify acts done.
  • Art 35: Power to make laws giving effect to specific FRs (16(3), 32(3), 33, 34) and punishing offences under Art 17 and Art 23 belongs ONLY to Parliament (not State Legislatures).

10. Important Judicial Precedents & Examples
  • Evolution of Art 21 (Procedure vs Due Process): In A.K. Gopalan (1950), court held that executive compliance with formal law is sufficient. In Maneka Gandhi (1978), SC expanded this: procedure itself must be just, fair, and reasonable, incorporating American Due Process into Indian law.
  • Creamy Layer & Reservation Cap: In Indra Sawhney (1992), SC upheld backward class quotas under 16(4) but capped reservations at 50% and excluded the creamy layer.
  • Involuntary Scientific Tests as Self-Incrimination: In Selvi v. State of Karnataka, SC ruled that involuntary narco-analysis and lie-detector tests violate the protection against self-incrimination under Art 20(3).
11. Exam-Trick Boxes (Avoid Common Traps!)
  • TRAP 1: "Article 19 is suspended during all emergencies." ➔ WRONG! Under Art 358, Art 19 is suspended only in external emergencies (war or external aggression). During an internal emergency (armed rebellion), Art 19 cannot be suspended!
  • TRAP 2: "Article 30 protects ethnic and racial minorities." ➔ WRONG! Article 30 exclusively covers Religious and Linguistic minorities only! (No recognition for ethnic, racial, or regional minorities).
  • TRAP 3: "Fundamental Rights are enforceable only against the State." ➔ WRONG! Articles 15(2) (access to public shops/wells), 17 (untouchability), 23 (trafficking/begar), and 24 (hazardous child labour) are enforceable directly against private individuals too!
  • TRAP 4: "National Awards like Bharat Ratna are titles prohibited under Art 18." ➔ WRONG! In Balaji Raghavan (1996), SC held they are valid constitutional decorations, but they cannot be used as prefixes or suffixes to the recipient's name.
  • TRAP 5: "Article 32 can be invoked for ordinary legal rights." ➔ WRONG! Art 32 is strictly limited to Part III Fundamental Rights. For ordinary legal or statutory rights, approach the High Court under Art 226.
  • TRAP 6: "The 50% reservation ceiling bars the 10% EWS quota." ➔ WRONG! In Janhit Abhiyan (2022), SC held that the 50% ceiling from Indra Sawhney applies strictly to caste-based reservations (SEBCs/SCs/STs) under 15(4) and 16(4), not to economic criteria under 15(6) and 16(6).
  • TRAP 7: "Fundamental Rights can never be amended." ➔ WRONG! Parliament can amend any Fundamental Right under Art 368 provided the amendment does not violate the Basic Structure of the Constitution (Kesavananda Bharati, 1973).
  • TRAP 8: "A person can claim reservation benefits by marrying into an SC/ST family or obtaining a post-marriage community certificate." ➔ WRONG!
    ▫ Constitutional Mandate: Caste and tribal status are strictly determined by birth and lifelong disadvantage, not by marriage, relocation, or voluntary association.
    ▫ Recent Regional Precedent (Adilabad ST Reserved Seat — Godam Nagesh v. Soyam Bapu Rao / Athram Sakku & Atram Suguna Controversy):
      • In electoral disputes arising from the Adilabad (ST) Parliamentary / Assembly constituencies, non-tribal women who married Scheduled Tribe (Gond / Pardhan) men claimed ST status and contested reserved elections on the basis of social acceptance into the husband's tribe.
      • Legal Verdict: The High Court and Scrutiny Committees, upholding the constitutional mandate of the Supreme Court in Valsamma Paul (1996) and Sunita Singh v. State of UP (2018), reiterated that a non-tribal woman does not acquire ST status by marrying a tribal man. Consequently, caste certificates obtained on the basis of marriage were declared invalid, and candidates face disqualification from contesting seats reserved under Articles 330 and 332.
12. Prelims Practice & Previous Year Questions (PYQs)
APPSC / UPSC High-Yield Trap
Q1. Under which Article of the Constitution of India is reservation in primary school education explicitly provided?
1) Article 15
2) Article 15(3)
3) Article 15(4)
4) None of the above
Show Answer & Constitutional Catch
➔ Correct Answer: 4) None of the above
▫ Explanation: The Constitution does not contain an explicit clause for "reservation" in primary school education. The mandatory 25% quota for disadvantaged and EWS children in private unaided schools is a statutory provision under Section 12(1)(c) of the RTE Act, 2009, which operationalizes Article 21A (Free and Compulsory Elementary Education).
▫ Crucial Difference: Articles 15(4) and 15(5) apply to admissions in higher educational institutions (colleges and professional degree seats), not primary schools.
APPSC Group-1 Prelims Standard
Q2. Under which constitutional provision is reservation for women in public employment legally enabled?
1) Article 16(2)
2) Article 16(4)
3) Article 15(3)
4) Article 16(3)
Show Answer & Constitutional Catch
➔ Correct Answer: 3) Article 15(3)
▫ Explanation: Article 16 has NO provision for women's reservation; Art 16(4) applies solely to socially and educationally backward classes lacking adequate representation.
▫ Judicial Benchmark: In Govt. of Andhra Pradesh v. P.B. Vijaykumar (1995), the Supreme Court ruled that Article 15(3) ("special provisions for women and children") empowers the State to create job reservations for women across government services.
▫ Vertical vs Horizontal: SC/ST/OBC quotas under 16(4) are Vertical Reservations, whereas women's quotas operate as Horizontal Reservations cutting across all vertical categories.

1) Life Insurance Corporation (LIC)
2) National Council of Educational Research and Training (NCERT)
3) Oil and Natural Gas Corporation (ONGC)
4) Delhi Municipal Corporation
Show Answer & Constitutional Catch
➔ Correct Answer: 2) NCERT
▫ Explanation: In Chander Mohan Khanna v. NCERT (1991), the Supreme Court ruled that NCERT is an autonomous registered society for academic research and training without governmental monopoly or deep pervasive state control; thus, it is not an instrumentality of the State under Article 12.
▫ Other Exclusions to Remember: BCCI (Zee Telefilms, 2005) and purely private un-aided educational bodies are also excluded from Article 12.

• Conclusion: Emphasize that affirmative action has expanded from rectifying historical social injustice to addressing multi-dimensional modern economic vulnerabilities.
