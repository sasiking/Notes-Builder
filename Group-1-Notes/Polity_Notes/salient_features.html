UPSC / APPSC POLITY MASTER BLUEPRINT

Salient Features, Schedules & Parts of the Indian Constitution

Philosophical Foundations • Structural Architecture • Exhaustive Framework
I. Structural Nature & Foundational Synthesis

1. Lengthiest Written Constitution

  • Original (1949): Preamble, 395 Articles (22 Parts), 8 Schedules[cite: 5].
  • Present: ~470 Articles[Including sub-clauses] (25 Parts), 12 Schedules[cite: 5].
    • ▫ Causative Factors: Vast geographical diversity, historical weight of the Government of India Act 1935, single constitution for both Union and States, dominance of legal luminaries in the Constituent Assembly[cite: 5].
📌 Elephantine Size Catch: Unlike the US Constitution (7 articles) or Australia (128 sections), India embedded administrative details, civil service protections (Part XIV), and emergency mechanics directly into the core text[cite: 5].

3. Blend of Rigidity & Flexibility

  • Article 368 Mechanisms: Synthesizes rigid amendment processes (US model) with flexible ones (UK model)[cite: 5].
    • ▫ Special Majority (Art 368): 50% total membership + 2/3rd present and voting (e.g., Fundamental Rights, DPSP)[cite: 5].
    ▫ Special Majority + State Ratification: Requires consent of half the state legislatures (e.g., federal provisions, GST Council)[cite: 5].
    ▫ Simple Majority (Outside 368): Articles 2, 3, 4 (creation/reorganization of states), official languages, citizenship rules[cite: 5].

2. Drawn from Diverse Sources

  • Dr. Ambedkar: Framed after "ransacking all the known constitutions of the world."[cite: 5]
    • ▫ Structural Backbone: Government of India Act 1935 (Federal Scheme, Office of Governor, Judiciary, Public Service Commissions, Emergency Provisions)[cite: 5].
    ▫ Philosophical Core: American (Fundamental Rights, Judicial Review) & Irish (DPSP, nomination of Rajya Sabha members)[cite: 5].
    ▫ Political Machinery: British Constitution (Parliamentary form, Rule of Law, Cabinet system, Prerogative writs, Single citizenship)[cite: 5].
📌 Critique vs. Reality: Often labeled a "borrowed constitution" or "patchwork," but the Framers deliberately modified overseas models to eliminate systemic flaws and adapt them to Indian conditions[cite: 5].

4. Federal System with Unitary Bias

  • Federal Traits: Two governments, division of powers (Seventh Schedule), written constitution, supremacy of the constitution, independent judiciary, bicameralism[cite: 5].
  • Unitary / Non-Federal Traits: Strong Centre, single constitution, single citizenship, integrated judiciary, flexibility of constitution, emergency provisions, appointment of Governor by the Centre, All-India Services[cite: 5].
    • ▫ Article 1: Designates India as a "Union of States" (not a federation), signaling that states have no right to secede and the union is not the result of an agreement among states[cite: 5].
  • TRAP: "The term 'Federation' is an explicit, defined constitutional feature in Article 1." ➔ WRONG![cite: 5]
    ▫ Constitutional Reality: The word "Federation" is nowhere mentioned in the entire text of the Constitution[cite: 5].
    ▫ Jurisprudential Characterizations: • K.C. Wheare: "Quasi-federal"[cite: 5]
    • Morris Jones: "Bargaining federalism"[cite: 5]
    • Granville Austin: "Cooperative federalism"[cite: 5]
    • S.R. Bommai case (1994): Supreme Court definitively affirmed that federalism is part of the Basic Structure[cite: 5].
II. Institutional Architecture & Governance Systems
Feature Constitutional Mechanics Key Nuances / Precedents
Parliamentary Form of Government Executive is drawn from and directly accountable to the Legislature[cite: 5]. Built on collective responsibility of the Council of Ministers to the Lok Sabha (Art 75(3))[cite: 5]. British model adopted at both Centre & States[cite: 5]. Operates on coordination and cooperation rather than rigid presidential separation of powers[cite: 5].
Sovereignty vs. Supremacy Synthesis Synthesizes British Parliamentary Sovereignty with American Judicial Supremacy[cite: 5]. Supreme Court checks laws via Judicial Review (Arts 13, 32, 226); Parliament retains authority to amend the constitution within the Basic Structure envelope[cite: 5].
Integrated & Independent Judiciary Single pyramid hierarchy enforcing both Union and State laws (SC ➔ High Courts ➔ Subordinate Courts)[cite: 5]. Distinct from dual US court systems[cite: 5]. Security of tenure, expenses charged on the Consolidated Fund of India, ban on post-retirement practice, power to punish for contempt (Art 129/215)[cite: 5].
Universal Adult Suffrage (Art 326) Democratic voting basis with zero communal or property qualifications[cite: 5]. Expanded via the 61st CAA (1988), reducing the voting age from 21 to 18[cite: 5]. Described as a bold experiment given India’s literacy rates and poverty levels during adoption[cite: 5].
Integrated Tier-3 Governance 73rd & 74th CAAs (1992): Constitutionalized Panchayati Raj (Part IX, 11th Sched) and Municipalities (Part IX-A, 12th Sched)[cite: 5]. Transforms a dual-polity system into a formal Three-Tier Federal Structure unique among world federations[cite: 5].
III. Rights, Directives & Secular Character

Fundamental Rights (Part III)

  • Magna Carta of India: Enforceable, justiciable rights against the State (Articles 12–35)[cite: 5].
  • Current 6 Categories:
    ▫ Right to Equality (14–18)[cite: 5]
    ▫ Right to Freedom (19–22)[cite: 5]
    ▫ Right against Exploitation (23–24)[cite: 5]
    ▫ Freedom of Religion (25–28)[cite: 5]
    ▫ Cultural and Educational Rights (29–30)[cite: 5]
    ▫ Right to Constitutional Remedies (Art 32 — "Heart and Soul")[cite: 5]

    • Check on State Power: Fundamental rights are not absolute; they are subject to reasonable restrictions (public order, morality, security of state)[cite: 5]. The Right to Property (Art 31) was stripped from Part III via the 44th CAA (1978) and made a legal right under Article 300A[cite: 5].

Fundamental Duties (Part IV-A)

  • Origin: Absent in original draft; added via 42nd CAA (1976) on Swaran Singh Committee recommendations[cite: 5].
  • Source: USSR Constitution[cite: 5].
  • Scope (Art 51A): Started with 10 duties; 11th duty added by 86th CAA (2002) (education for children 6–14)[cite: 5].
    • ▫ Nature: Non-justiciable moral obligations; serve as interpretive guides for constitutional validity of statutes[cite: 5].

Directive Principles (Part IV)

  • Instruments of Instructions: Non-justiciable socio-economic targets for the State (Articles 36–51)[cite: 5].
  • Granville Austin: DPSP + Fundamental Rights constitute the "Conscience of the Constitution."[cite: 5]
    • ▫ Three Categories (Ideological): Socialistic (Arts 38, 39), Gandhian (Arts 40, 43, 47), Liberal-Intellectual (Arts 44, 45, 50, 51)[cite: 5].
📌 Minerva Mills Case (1980): The Indian Constitution is founded on the bedrock of the balance between Fundamental Rights and Directive Principles[cite: 5]. Neither is absolute over the other[cite: 5].

Positive Concept of Secularism

  • No State Religion: Equal respect and protection for all religions (Sarva Dharma Sambhava)[cite: 5].
    • ▫ Western vs. Indian Secularism: Western model enforces strict, mutual non-intervention between Church and State[cite: 5]. Indian model practices principled intervention (e.g., banning untouchability under Art 17, reforming Hindu religious trusts under Art 25(2)(b))[cite: 5].
    ▫ Preamble: The term "Secular" was inserted into the Preamble by the 42nd CAA (1976), though implicitly codified from 1950 via Articles 25–28[cite: 5].
IV. Emergency Machinery & Independent Safeguards

Emergency Provisions (Part XVIII)

  • Article 352 (National Emergency): War, external aggression, or armed rebellion (substituted "internal disturbance" via 44th CAA)[cite: 5].
  • Article 356 (President's Rule): Breakdown of constitutional machinery in states[cite: 5].
  • Article 360 (Financial Emergency): Threat to financial stability/credit of India (never invoked)[cite: 5].
    • ▫ Federal Transformation: Converts federal arrangement into a unitary structure without formal constitutional amendment[cite: 5].

Independent Watchdog Bodies

  • Election Commission (Art 324): Free and fair elections to Parliament, State Legislatures, President, and VP[cite: 5].
  • Comptroller and Auditor General (Art 148): Guardian of the public purse[cite: 5].
  • UPSC & State PSCs (Arts 315–323): Merit-based recruitment watchdogs for administrative machinery[cite: 5].
    • ▫ Protected via fixed tenures, charged expenditures, and removal mechanisms aligned with Supreme Court judges[cite: 5].
V. Schedules of the Indian Constitution (1 to 12)

Memory Engine: "TEARS OF OLD PM"

The original 1949 Constitution contained 8 Schedules[cite: 5]. Through subsequent constitutional amendments, 4 additional schedules were added, taking the current tally to 12 Schedules[cite: 5]:

  • T - 1st: Territories & States (Arts 1 & 4)
  • E - 2nd: Emoluments, Salaries & Perks (Arts 59, 65, 75, 97, 125, 148, 158, 164, 186, 221)
  • A - 3rd: Affirmations & Oaths (Arts 75, 84, 99, 124, 146, 173, 188, 219)
  • R - 4th: Rajya Sabha Seat Allocation (Arts 4 & 80)
  • S - 5th: Scheduled Areas & Scheduled Tribes (Art 244(1))
  • O - 6th: Other Tribal Areas [AMTM states] (Arts 244(2) & 275(1))
  • F - 7th: Federal Legislative Lists [Union, State, Concurrent] (Art 246)
  • O - 8th: Official Recognised Languages [22 languages] (Arts 344(1) & 351)
  • L - 9th: Land Reforms & Regulatory Validity [1st CAA, 1951] (Art 31B)
  • D - 10th: Defection Disqualification [52nd CAA, 1985] (Arts 102(2) & 191(2))
  • P - 11th: Panchayat Powers & 29 Functional Matters [73rd CAA, 1992] (Art 243G)[cite: 5]
  • M - 12th: Municipalities & 18 Functional Matters [74th CAA, 1992] (Art 243W)[cite: 5]
Schedule Subject Matter Constitutional Scope & Provisions Exam Traps & CAAs Linkage
First Schedule States & Union Territories Names of the 28 States and 8 UTs along with their territorial jurisdictions and boundary extents (Arts 1 & 4). Any change in state boundaries under Art 3 automatically amends the 1st Schedule via Simple Majority outside Art 368[cite: 5].
Second Schedule Salaries & Emoluments Covers President, Governors, Speaker/Dy. Speaker (LS & SLA), Chairman/Dy. Chairman (RS & SLC), SC/HC Judges, and CAG. TRAP: Prime Minister, Union Ministers, MPs, and Chief Ministers are NOT listed in the 2nd Schedule (their salaries are fixed by Parliament law).
Third Schedule Forms of Oaths or Affirmations Covers Union/State Ministers, MP/MLA candidates, MPs/MLAs, SC/HC Judges, and CAG. TRAP: Oaths of the President (Art 60), Vice-President (Art 69), and Governor (Art 159) are specified in their respective articles, not in the 3rd Schedule.
Fourth Schedule Allocation of Rajya Sabha Seats Allots seats to States and UTs on the basis of population (Arts 4(1) & 80(2)). Only 3 UTs currently have representation in Rajya Sabha: Delhi (3), Puducherry (1), and Jammu & Kashmir (4).
Fifth Schedule Scheduled Areas & ST Administration Deals with control and administration of Scheduled Areas and Scheduled Tribes in states other than the 4 northeastern states (Art 244(1)). Requires constitution of a Tribes Advisory Council (TAC) (up to 20 members). Governor can direct that an Act of Parliament/State legislature does not apply.
Sixth Schedule Northeastern Tribal Areas (AMTM) Autonomous District Councils (ADCs) and Regional Councils in 4 specific states: Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram (Arts 244(2) & 275(1)). TRAP: Manipur, Nagaland, and Arunachal Pradesh are NOT under the 6th Schedule. ADCs have legislative, judicial, and taxing powers under Governor's assent.
Seventh Schedule Division of Legislative Powers Three legislative lists (Art 246):
• Union List: 100 items (originally 97)
• State List: 61 items (originally 66)
• Concurrent List: 52 items (originally 47) 		42nd CAA (1976) transferred 5 subjects from State to Concurrent List: Education, Forests, Weights & Measures, Protection of Wild Animals/Birds, and Administration of Justice. Residual powers belong to Centre (Art 248).
Eighth Schedule Recognised Languages Lists the 22 official languages (originally 14) (Arts 344(1) & 351). Added via 3 Constitutional Amendments:
• 21st CAA (1967): Sindhi
• 71st CAA (1992): Konkani, Manipuri, Nepali (KMN)
• 92nd CAA (2003): Bodo, Dogri, Maithili, Santhali (BDMS)
TRAP: English and Rajasthani are NOT in the 8th Schedule.
Ninth Schedule Validation of Certain Acts & Regulations Added by 1st CAA (1951) to protect land reform laws from judicial scrutiny under Fundamental Rights (Art 31B). Contains ~284 laws. I.R. Coelho Case (2007): Supreme Court ruled that laws placed in the 9th Schedule after April 24, 1973 (Kesavananda Bharati verdict date) are open to Judicial Review if they violate the Basic Structure.
Tenth Schedule Anti-Defection Law Added by 52nd CAA (1985); sets disqualification grounds for legislators defecting from political parties (Arts 102(2) & 191(2)). • 91st CAA (2003): Omitted the split defense (1/3rd split no longer valid; only 2/3rd merger is protected).
• Kihoto Hollohan (1992): Presiding Officer's decision is subject to judicial review.
Eleventh Schedule Panchayati Raj Functional Domains Added by 73rd CAA (1992); enumerates 29 functional items placed within the purview of Panchayats (Art 243G)[cite: 5]. Includes agriculture, minor irrigation, animal husbandry, rural housing, drinking water, poverty alleviation, and primary/secondary schools.
Twelfth Schedule Municipal Functional Domains Added by 74th CAA (1992); enumerates 18 functional items placed within the purview of Urban Local Bodies (Art 243W)[cite: 5]. Includes urban planning, regulation of land use, roads, bridges, water supply, public health, fire services, and slum improvement.
VI. Parts of the Indian Constitution (Original 22 vs. Present 25)

Structural Evolution of Parts

Originally in 1949, the Constitution was divided into 22 Parts[cite: 5]. Through subsequent constitutional amendments:

  • 1 Part Deleted: Part VII (dealing with Part B States) was deleted by the 7th CAA (1956).
  • 4 Parts Added:
    ▫ Part IV-A (Fundamental Duties) — inserted by 42nd CAA (1976)[cite: 5].
    ▫ Part IX-A (The Municipalities) — inserted by 74th CAA (1992)[cite: 5].
    ▫ Part IX-B (The Co-operative Societies) — inserted by 97th CAA (2011).
    ▫ Part XIV-A (Tribunals) — inserted by 42nd CAA (1976).
  • Result: 22 - 1 + 4 = 25 Parts in numerical enumeration, though the last numbered part remains numbered up to XXII[cite: 5].

Part I The Union and its Territory Articles 1 to 4 Art 1 (Union of States)[cite: 5], Art 2 (Admission/establishment of new states), Art 3 (Alteration of areas, boundaries or names of existing states by simple majority)[cite: 5].

Part VIII The Union Territories Articles 239 to 242 Administration by President through Administrator/LG; Art 239AA (Special provisions with respect to National Capital Territory of Delhi - 69th CAA, 1991).
Part IX The Panchayats Articles 243 to 243-O Constitutionalized by 73rd CAA (1992)[cite: 5]; 3-tier Panchayati Raj system, State Election Commission (243K), State Finance Commission (243I).
Part IX-A The Municipalities Articles 243-P to 243-ZG Constitutionalized by 74th CAA (1992)[cite: 5]; Nagar Panchayats, Municipal Councils, Municipal Corporations, District Planning Committees (243ZD).

Part X The Scheduled and Tribal Areas Articles 244 to 244A Administration of 5th Schedule (Scheduled Areas) and 6th Schedule (Tribal Areas in Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura, Mizoram).
Part XI Relations between Union and States Articles 245 to 263 • Ch I: Legislative Relations (Territorial & Subject-matter distribution - Arts 245–255)
• Ch II: Administrative Relations (Inter-State Waters - Art 262, Inter-State Council - Art 263).
Part XII Finance, Property, Contracts & Suits Articles 264 to 300A • Ch I: Finance (Consolidated/Contingency Funds, Finance Commission - Art 280, GST Council - Art 279A)
• Ch II: Borrowing
• Ch III: Property, Contracts, Rights, Liabilities
• Ch IV: Right to Property (Article 300A) — shifted from Part III by 44th CAA[cite: 5].
Part XIII Trade, Commerce & Intercourse within India Articles 301 to 307 Freedom of inter-state trade and commerce (Art 301 inspired by Australian Constitution); Parliament's power to impose restrictions in public interest.
Part XIV Services Under the Union and States Articles 308 to 323 • Ch I: Services (All-India Services - Art 312, Doctrine of Pleasure - Art 310, Protections - Art 311)
• Ch II: Public Service Commissions (UPSC & SPSC - Arts 315–323)[cite: 5].
Part XIV-A Tribunals Articles 323A & 323B Inserted by 42nd CAA (1976) based on Swaran Singh Committee; Art 323A (Administrative Tribunals - CAT/SAT), Art 323B (Tribunals for other matters - taxation, tenancy, labor).
Part XV Elections Articles 324 to 329A Art 324 (Election Commission of India superintendence and control)[cite: 5]; Art 325 (No discrimination in electoral rolls); Art 326 (Universal Adult Suffrage)[cite: 5].
Part XVI Special Provisions Relating to Certain Classes Articles 330 to 342A Reservations for SCs and STs in Lok Sabha (Art 330) and State Assemblies (Art 332); National Commissions: NCSC (Art 338), NCST (Art 338A), NCBC (Art 338B - 102nd CAA).
Part XVII Official Language Articles 343 to 351 • Ch I: Language of the Union (Hindi in Devanagari script - Art 343)
• Ch II: Regional Languages
• Ch III: Language of SC and HCs (English until Parliament laws - Art 348)
• Ch IV: Special Directives (Art 350A, Art 351).
Part XVIII Emergency Provisions Articles 352 to 360 Art 352 (National Emergency), Art 356 (State Emergency / President's Rule), Art 360 (Financial Emergency), Arts 358 & 359 (Suspension of Fundamental Rights)[cite: 5].
Part XIX Miscellaneous Articles 361 to 367 Art 361 (Immunities and protections to President and Governors from criminal prosecution while in office); definition of terms.
Part XX Amendment of the Constitution Article 368 Constituent power of Parliament to amend the Constitution and procedure thereof; requires Special Majority / State Ratification[cite: 5]; subject to Basic Structure[cite: 5].
Part XXI Temporary, Transitional and Special Provisions Articles 369 to 392 Special asymmetric provisions for certain states: Art 371 (Maharashtra/Gujarat), 371A (Nagaland), 371B (Assam), 371C (Manipur), 371D/E (AP/Telangana), 371F (Sikkim), 371G (Mizoram), 371H (Arunachal), 371-I (Goa), 371J (Karnataka). (Art 370 effectively abrogated in 2019).
Part XXII Short Title, Commencement, Authoritative Text in Hindi and Repeals Articles 393 to 395 Art 393 ("This Constitution may be called the Constitution of India"); Art 394 (Commencement); Art 394A (Authoritative text in Hindi - added by 58th CAA, 1987); Art 395 (Repeals of Indian Independence Act 1947 & GoI Act 1935).
  • HIGH-YIELD TRAP: Numerical Numbering vs. Sub-clause Parts ➔ MUST KNOW!
    ▫ Common Error: Claiming the Constitution ends at Part XXV.
    ▫ Constitutional Fact: The final numbered Part of the Indian Constitution is still Part XXII (Article 395). The 4 additions (IV-A, IX-A, IX-B, XIV-A) were added alphabetically to maintain the original numbering framework[cite: 5].
    ▫ Chandra Kumar Case (1997): SC held that the power of judicial review under Arts 226/227 and 32 is an integral part of the Basic Structure. Hence, the exclusion of High Court jurisdiction under Part XIV-A (Art 323A/B) was declared unconstitutional.

Mains Practice • 15 Marks Q1. "The Indian Constitution is an organic document that balances functional stability with dynamic adaptability." Analyze this statement with reference to the interplay between the Basic Structure Doctrine and the amending powers under Article 368[cite: 5].
View Model Answer Structuring Framework
Answer Framework:[cite: 5]
• Introduction: Define an "organic document" as one that grows and evolves while maintaining core constitutional continuity[cite: 5]. Cite Justice Khanna's observation that the Constitution is a vehicle of a nation’s life, not a static legal text[cite: 5].
• Dynamic Adaptability (Article 368): Detail how over 105 constitutional amendments have addressed emerging socio-economic demands (e.g., 1st CAA for land reforms, 73rd/74th CAAs for local self-governance, 101st CAA for GST, 103rd CAA for EWS)[cite: 5].
• Functional Stability (The Basic Structure Shield): Trace the judicial evolution from Shankari Prasad (1951) and Golaknath (1967) to the landmark Kesavananda Bharati (1973) verdict[cite: 5]. Explain how the doctrine sets boundary markers (Rule of Law, Federalism, Secularism, Judicial Review, Separation of Powers) that prevent majoritarian overreach[cite: 5].
• The Interplay: The doctrine does not halt amendments; it channels them[cite: 5]. Contrast India's stable constitutional survival with neighbors that rewrote constitutions entirely when political systems fractured[cite: 5].
• Conclusion: Reiterate that the synthesis of flexible amendment powers and the basic structure boundary creates an equilibrium: keeping the Constitution durable against erosion without rendering it brittle to changing societal values[cite: 5].
Mains Practice • 15 Marks Q2. "The structural compartmentalization of the Constitution into Parts and Schedules reflects an asymmetric federal architecture and a calibrated balance between administrative governance and judicial accountability." Discuss with examples.
View Model Answer Structuring Framework
Answer Framework:
• Introduction: Note how the Constitution departs from traditional symmetric federations by integrating unique governance charters across 25 Parts and 12 Schedules[cite: 5].
• Asymmetric Federal Architecture: ▫ Part XXI (Articles 371 to 371-J): Accommodates regional socio-cultural specificities (e.g., Article 371A for Nagaland protects customary laws; 371D for Andhra Pradesh education quotas).
▫ 5th & 6th Schedules (Part X): Tailored tribal self-rule; Autonomous District Councils in AMTM states enjoy legislative autonomy distinct from mainstream local governance under Part IX & IX-A[cite: 5].
• Administrative Governance vs. Judicial Check: ▫ Part XIV & XIV-A: Balance public servant security (Art 311) with specialized dispute resolution tribunals (Arts 323A/B), subject to High Court review per L. Chandra Kumar.
▫ Part XII (Article 300A): Transition of the right to property from a fundamental right to a constitutional right strikes a balance between eminent domain for development and individual safeguards against arbitrary deprivation[cite: 5].
• Conclusion: The division of the Constitution into detailed Parts and Schedules acts as an institutional roadmap, balancing administrative flexibility with rule-of-law constraints.
