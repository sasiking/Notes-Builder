I. Demarcating Space & Global Governance

1. Demarcating Outer Space (The Kármán Line)

  • Definition: Imaginary line located at 100 km (62 miles) above sea level that demarcates Earth's atmosphere from outer space.
  • Origin: Established in the 1960s by the Fédération Aéronautique Internationale (FAI); named after aerospace pioneer Theodore von Kármán.
  • Aerodynamic Principle: Marks the boundary where air becomes too thin for aircraft wings to generate aerodynamic lift.
    ▫ Vehicles above this line must rely on rocket propulsion systems rather than aerodynamic lift.
    ▫ Anyone crossing this altitude qualifies as an astronaut.
  • Alternative Boundaries: US FAA, NASA, and US military define outer space starting at 80 km (50 miles). Most nations adhere to the 100 km standard.
  • Legal Status: Outer space lacks national sovereignty; acts like international waters under the 1967 Outer Space Treaty to prevent geopolitical claims.

2. Global Treaties & Artemis Accords

  • UN Institutional Framework: Established in 1958 by UNGA: UN COPUOS (Committee on the Peaceful Uses of Outer Space), assisted by UNOOSA (UN Office for Outer Space Affairs).
  • The 5 Core UN Space Treaties:
    ▫ Outer Space Treaty (1967): Peaceful uses; prohibits WMDs in orbit; open exploration.
    ▫ Rescue Agreement (1968): Safe recovery and repatriation of astronauts.
    ▫ Liability Convention (1972): Absolute state liability for damage caused by space objects.
    ▫ Registration Convention (1976): Mandatory registry of launched space objects.
    ▫ Moon Agreement (1979): Declares the Moon and its natural resources common heritage of mankind.
  • India's Stance: Signatory to all 5 treaties; ratified 4; has NOT ratified the 1979 Moon Agreement.
  • Artemis Accords (2020): Led by NASA and US Dept of State with 7 founding members (Australia, Canada, Italy, Japan, Luxembourg, UAE, UK). Non-binding civil principles for Moon, Mars, comets, and asteroids. India is a signatory nation (47 signatory nations total).
II. Physics of Spaceflight & Orbital Mechanics

1. Orbital Equilibrium: Gravitational vs. Centrifugal Balance

A satellite remains in orbit when the inward pull of Earth's gravity is counterbalanced by the outward centrifugal force created by its orbital velocity.

Figure: Equilibrium of Forces in a Circular Orbit

Orbital Velocity (Vo)

Vo = √(g · R)
Velocity needed to keep a satellite in circular orbit around Earth.

Escape Velocity (Ve)

Ve = √(2 · g · R) = √2 · Vo ≈ 1.414 · Vo
Escape velocity is √2 times (~41% greater than) orbital velocity. Earth: 11.2 km/s; Moon: 2.4 km/s. Independent of body mass, shape, or launch angle. Also called Second Cosmic Velocity.

2. Kepler's Three Laws of Planetary Motion

  • First Law (Law of Orbits): The orbit of a planet is an ellipse with the Sun located at one of the two focal points (foci). Most satellites follow elliptical orbits.
  • Second Law (Law of Equal Areas): A line connecting a satellite to the center of Earth sweeps out equal areas in equal intervals of time.
    ▫ Velocity is non-uniform: Fastest at Perigee (closest to Earth) and Slowest at Apogee (farthest from Earth).
  • Third Law (Law of Periods): The square of the orbital period is directly proportional to the cube of the semi-major axis of its orbit (T2 ∝ a3).
Figure: Kepler's 1st & 2nd Laws (Elliptical Orbits & Equal Swept Areas)

3. Orbital Geometry & Parameters

  • Eccentricity (e): Measures orbit roundness (0 ≤ e < 1).
    ▫ e = 0: Perfect circle.
    ▫ 0 < e < 1: Elliptical orbit (e.g., Molniya orbit uses e = 0.722).
  • Orbital Inclination: Angle between the orbital plane and Earth's equator.
    ▫ 0°: Equatorial orbit.
    ▫ ~90°: Polar orbit.
  • Direction of Rotation:
    ▫ Prograde: Moves in the same direction as Earth's rotation (West to East).
    ▫ Retrograde: Moves opposite to Earth's rotation (East to West).

4. Lagrange Points (L-Points) & Halo Orbits

Five equilibrium points in space where gravitational forces between Sun and Earth balance out the centripetal motion of a satellite (discovered by Joseph-Louis Lagrange).

Figure: Sun-Earth Lagrange System Geometry (L1 to L5)
  • L1 (Between Sun and Earth): Uninterrupted view of the Sun without eclipses. Home to Aditya-L1 in a Halo orbit.
  • L2 (Behind Earth, facing deep space): Shielded from solar and terrestrial glare. Host to the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST).
  • L3 (Opposite side of Sun): Directly behind the Sun, completely hidden from Earth.
  • L4 & L5 (Equilateral Apexes): Gravitationally stable regions located 60° ahead and behind Earth's orbit.
IV. Functional Types of Satellites
Figure: Principle of Satellite Communications via Onboard Transponders

1. Communication Satellites

  • Core Mechanism: Equipped with transponders that receive uplink signals from ground stations, convert frequencies, amplify power, and retransmit via downlinks.
  • Key Transponder Bands: C-band, Extended C-band, Ku-band, and Ka-band.
  • Orbits Deployed:
    ▫ Geostationary Orbit (GEO): Wide-area coverage, stationary ground tracking, stable connection. Disadvantage: high round-trip latency (~477 ms). Examples: INSAT and GSAT series.
    ▫ Low Earth Orbit (LEO): Very low latency (2–27 ms), higher bandwidth, enables high-speed internet. Requires large satellite constellations. Examples: SpaceX Starlink (4,200+ sats), OneWeb (648 planned; 36 launched by ISRO).
  • Applications: Telecommunications, direct-to-home (DTH) TV, disaster warnings, search and rescue, VSAT, and rural connectivity.

2. Earth Observation Satellites (EOS / Remote Sensing)

  • Working Principle: Collects spatial, spectral, and temporal data by measuring reflected and emitted electromagnetic radiation from Earth's landscapes.
  • Orbit: Follows a synchronous sub-recurrent polar orbit (Sun-Synchronous Orbit), ensuring identical solar illumination at fixed local solar times.
  • Imaging Modes:
    ▫ Optical / Multispectral: High-resolution surface imaging in visible, NIR, and SWIR bands (e.g., Cartosat, Resourcesat, HysIS).
    ▫ Synthetic Aperture Radar (SAR): Active microwave sensors (C-band, X-band) providing day-and-night, all-weather, cloud-penetrating imagery (e.g., RISAT-1, RISAT-2, EOS-04).
  • Applications: Crop yield assessment, mineral prospecting, urban and town planning via GIS, deforestation tracking, shoreline change, and disaster assessment.

3. Navigation Satellites

  • Constellation Architecture: Constellations of satellites placed in MEO (~20,000 km) or combined GEO/GSO orbits continuously broadcasting time, position, and ephemeris signals.
  • Precision Clocks: Utilize onboard atomic clocks (rubidium or passive hydrogen masers) for sub-microsecond timing precision.
  • Two Major Classes:
    ▫ Global (GNSS): GPS (US), GLONASS (Russia), Galileo (EU), BeiDou (China).
    ▫ Regional (RNSS): NavIC (India - 7 satellites (3 in Geostationary, 4 in geosynchronous orbit) covering India + 1,500 km)
    • QZSS (Japan - 4 satellites in quasi-zenith orbits).
  • Satellite-Based Augmentation Systems (SBAS): Systems like India's GAGAN (ISRO + AAI) transmitting correction signals via GEO transponders (GSAT-8, GSAT-10) for aviation approach safety.

4. Astronomical Satellites (Space Observatories)

  • Purpose: Giant telescopes operating above Earth's atmospheric distortion, absorption, and light pollution to observe distant cosmic phenomena.
  • Multi-Wavelength Regimes:
    ▫ Optical & Ultraviolet: Hubble Space Telescope (547 km LEO; serviceable by astronauts).
    ▫ Infrared: James Webb Space Telescope (Sun-Earth L2; explores the earliest stars and universe phases); Spitzer Space Telescope.
    ▫ X-Ray: Chandra X-ray Observatory; ISRO's XPoSat (measuring cosmic X-ray polarization).
    ▫ Multi-Wavelength Simultaneous: India's AstroSat (first dedicated Indian astronomy observatory operating across X-ray, Optical, and UV bands).

GPS-Aided GEO Augmented Navigation (GAGAN) & NavIC Applications

  • GAGAN (Satellite Based Augmentation System - SBAS):
    ▫ Jointly developed by ISRO and the Airports Authority of India (AAI) to establish satellite-based navigation over Indian airspace for civil aviation.
    ▫ Transmits Signal-In-Space (SIS) through communication payloads hosted on GSAT-8 and GSAT-10.
    ▫ Interoperable with international SBAS networks; GEO footprint spans seamlessly from Africa to Australia.
    ▫ Extends beyond civil aviation to smart transport, railway signaling, marine navigation, surveying, geodesy, and telecom networks.
  • Applications of NavIC (IRNSS):
    ▫ Terrestrial, Aerial & Marine Navigation: Waypoint tracking, port vessel docking, and deep-sea route optimization.
    ▫ Disaster Management: Targeted cyclone and tsunami alert dissemination directly to fishermen vessels beyond cellular range.
    ▫ Vehicle Tracking & Fleet Management: Real-time public transit logistics, freight corridor tracking, and commercial carrier routing.
    ▫ Mobile & Driver Navigation: Integration into consumer smartphone chipsets for driver visual/voice assistance.
    ▫ Precise Timing: High-precision nanosecond time-stamping for telecommunication networks, power grids, and financial banking servers.
    ▫ Geodesy & Mapping: Cadastral boundary demarcation, topographical surveys, and geodetic data capture.
    ▫ Recreational Transit: Off-grid location assistance for hikers, explorers, and rural field personnel.

5. Climate & Environmental Research Satellites

  • Scope: Specialized sensors gathering multi-faceted data across the global ocean surface, polar ice sheets, atmosphere, and biosphere.
  • Focus Areas: Atmospheric water vapor cycles, ocean evaporation, cloud dynamics, greenhouse gas concentrations, and sea surface topography.
  • Prominent Examples:
    ▫ Aqua & the "A-Train" Constellation: NASA-led international Earth Observing System constellation tracking global water cycle dynamics.
    ▫ Megha-Tropiques: Joint Indo-French satellite tracking tropical water and energy cycles.
    ▫ SARAL: Indo-French satellite using the AltiKa radar altimeter for oceanographic topography.
    ▫ EUMETSAT: Operational European weather and environmental monitoring constellation.

6. Biosatellites (Biological Research in Orbit)

  • Scientific Role: Spacecraft designed to conduct microgravity and cosmic radiation experiments on living plant and animal cells, seeds, and biological structures.
  • Medical Value: Essential for understanding cellular bone density loss, muscular atrophy, genetic mutations, and pharmacology in space environments.
  • Benchmark Missions:
    ▫ Biosatellite Program (NASA): Early series analyzing spaceflight effects on biological specimens.
    ▫ Bion Space Program (Roscosmos): Specialized recoverable biosatellite series (e.g., Bion-M) conducting deep physiological research on live organisms.

1. Mass-Based Satellite Hierarchy

  • Large Satellites: > 1,000 kg
  • Medium Satellites: 500 to 1,000 kg
  • Small Satellites: < 500 kg
    ▫ Minisatellites: 100 to 500 kg
    ▫ Microsatellites: 10 to 100 kg
    ▫ Nanosatellites: 1 to 10 kg
    ▫ Picosatellites: 100 g to 1 kg
    ▫ Femtosatellites: 10 g to 100 g
    ▫ Attosatellites: 1 g to 10 g
    ▫ Zeptosatellites: 0.1 g to 1 g
  • CubeSats: From ~0.2 kg to ~40 kg (0.25U to 27U; 1U = 10 × 10 × 10 cm).
  • ISRO Small Satellite Buses: IMS-1 (~30 kg capacity); IMS-2 (~100 kg capacity).

2. NavIC: Architecture & Ground Footprint

  • Seven Eyes in the Sky: 7 operational satellites: 3 in Geostationary Orbit (GEO) (fixed longitudes) + 4 in inclined Geosynchronous Orbit (IGSO) (moving in pairs tracing a figure-8 path).
  • Clocks: 3 rubidium atomic clocks onboard each satellite for accurate positioning.
  • Coverage: Entire Indian territory + extends up to 1,500 km beyond national borders. Position accuracy better than 20 meters.
  • Services: Standard Positioning Service (SPS) for public/civilians; Restricted Service (RS) encrypted for defense/security.
  • Global IMO Recognition: India is the 4th nation to have an independent regional navigation system accepted into the World Wide Radio Navigation System (WWRNS).

VII. Advanced Propulsion: Ramjet, Scramjet & Cryogenics
Figure: Airflow and Combustion Mechanics: Ramjet vs. Scramjet Engines

1. Cryogenic Science

  • Physics: Liquid propellants stored and burned at temperatures below -150°C.
    ▫ Fuel: Liquid Hydrogen (LH2) at -253°C.
    ▫ Oxidizer: Liquid Oxygen (LOX) at -183°C.
  • Specific Impulse: Yields higher thrust per kilogram of propellant than solid or earth-storable chemical propellants.
  • Turbo-Pumps: High-speed propellant delivery (~30,000–40,000 rpm) to mix liquids in the combustion chamber.
  • Global Standing: India is the 6th country with proven cryogenic capability (US, Russia, France/ESA, Japan, China, India).

2. Cryogenic Historical Timeline

  • 1986: ₹12-crore project started to master cryogenic fluid handling.
  • 1991–1993: Deal with Russian Glavkosmos stalled under US MTCR pressure; indigenous Cryogenic Upper Stage Project (CUSP) sanctioned (₹280 crore).
  • 2003: First successful static engine test.
  • 2010: GSLV-D3 failure due to cryogenic pump ignition issues.
  • Jan 5, 2014: First successful flight of GSLV-D5 placing GSAT-14 into orbit.

  • TRAP 1: "Escape velocity depends on the mass of the launched vehicle." ➔ FALSE! Formula is Ve = √(2 · g · R); it is entirely independent of the spacecraft's mass or launch angle.
  • TRAP 2: "Sun-Synchronous Orbits have zero degree inclination." ➔ FALSE! Equatorial orbits are 0°. SSO is a retrograde polar orbit inclined at roughly 98.2°.
  • TRAP 3: "India has ratified all five UN Space Treaties." ➔ FALSE! India signed all 5 but ratified only 4. India has NOT ratified the 1979 Moon Agreement.
  • TRAP 4: "Geostationary and Geosynchronous are identical terms." ➔ FALSE! Every Geostationary orbit is Geosynchronous, but not vice-versa. A Geostationary orbit strictly requires zero inclination and zero eccentricity right above the Equator.
  • TRAP 5: "Under the 2023 Policy, IN-SPACe sells satellite capacity." ➔ FALSE! NSIL is the commercial and manufacturing arm. IN-SPACe is the single-window promotional and regulatory authorization agency.
