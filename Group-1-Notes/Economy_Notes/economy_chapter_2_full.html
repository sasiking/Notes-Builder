Chapter 2: Resource Mobilization in Indian Economy

Budgetary Resources • Tax & Non-Tax Revenues • Public Debt • FDI & FII • DFI • Infrastructure & Energy Transition

Syllabus Scope & Core Themes

  • What is Resource Mobilization: Gathering and putting to work financial, human, and physical resources to drive economic growth and public welfare.
  • Core Areas Covered: Government budget planning, direct and indirect tax collections, non-tax incomes, domestic and foreign public debt, foreign investment routes (FDI vs. FII), financial institutions and specialized development banks (NABARD, SIDBI, IDFC), large infrastructure programs (Bharatmala, Sagarmala, DFCs, VCIC), and moving from fossil fuels to clean energy (Coal to Gas to Renewables to Green Hydrogen; Strategic Petroleum Reserves).
I. Budgetary Resources & The Union Budget

1. How the Union Budget is Built

  • Basic Meaning: The government's annual statement showing expected earnings and planned spending for the year.
  • Revenue Budget:
    ▫ Handles routine income and day-to-day spending.
    ▫ Does not create any physical or financial assets and does not cut down government debt.
    ▫ Includes personal and corporate taxes, GST, interest earned, fees, grants, and subsidies.
  • Capital Budget:
    ▫ Handles long-term capital receipts and capital spending.
    ▫ Receipts: Money raised from market loans, disinvestment of PSU shares, and recovering old loans.
    ▫ Expenditures: Money spent to build physical assets (highways, bridges) or financial assets (loans given to states).

2. Importance & Impact on the Economy

  • Guides Economic Planning: Helps decide how much public money goes to healthcare, schools, farmers, and roads.
  • Maintains Fiscal Discipline: Keeps public borrowing within safe limits to prevent runaway fiscal deficits.
  • Generates Jobs & Bridges Gaps: Heavy capital spending creates industrial work, while welfare spending supports low-income families.
  • Builds Investor Confidence: Predictable and balanced budgets reassure businesses and foreign rating agencies.
  • Connects with Monetary Policy: The amount the government borrows changes market interest rates, loan availability, and RBI liquidity actions.
II. Tax and Non-Tax Revenue Framework
Type of Tax Direct Tax Revenue Indirect Tax Revenue
Simple Meaning Paid directly by the person or company earning the income; the burden cannot be passed on to anyone else. Takes a higher share from the rich (progressive). Levied on goods and services; sellers add it to prices so the final buyer pays it. Can burden lower-income buyers if basic items are taxed high (regressive).
Main Examples • Personal Income Tax: Progressive tax paid by individuals based on income slabs.
• Corporate Tax: Tax paid by companies on their net profits.
• Wealth Tax: Scrapped in 2015 to simplify tax filing and cut down disputes. 		• Goods and Services Tax (GST): One unified tax across India replacing multiple old state and central taxes.
• Customs Duty: Tax on goods entering or leaving the country.
• Excise Duty: Tax on producing items outside GST, like petrol, diesel, and liquor.

Non-Tax Revenue Sources

  • Dividends & Profits: Surplus profit sent by the RBI, and dividends earned from government-owned companies (PSUs) like ONGC, IOCL, and SBI.
  • Interest & Service Fees: Interest on loans given to state governments, plus user fees collected for passport services, telecom spectrum licenses, mining rights, and penalties.
  • Grants-in-Aid: Direct grants received from international agencies or foreign governments that do not have to be paid back.
  • Asset Monetization: Raising revenue by leasing out public assets like railway tracks, roads, telecom towers, and empty government land to private operators.

Challenges in Collecting Revenue

  • Tax Evasion: Hiding real earnings and using illegal methods to avoid paying taxes causes large revenue losses.
  • Large Informal Sector: Many small businesses and workers deal only in cash, staying outside the formal tax net.
  • Complex Filing Rules: Difficult paperwork, shifting rules, and long court cases delay revenue recovery.
  • Risk of High Tax Rates: Setting taxes too high can hurt new business investments, reduce job creation, and push people to hide income.
III. Public Debt: Meaning, Types & Risks

1. What is Public Debt & How is it Divided?

  • Basic Concept: The total money borrowed by the government over time to cover budget shortfalls and fund big development works.
  • Internal Debt (Inside India):
    ▫ Marketable Debt: Government securities (G-Secs, Treasury Bills) that banks and institutions can freely trade in financial markets.
    ▫ Non-Marketable Debt: Non-tradable funds like post office small savings (NSSF), provident funds, and special securities issued to public bodies.
  • External Debt (From Abroad): Loans taken from foreign bodies—like the World Bank, Asian Development Bank, foreign governments, or foreign commercial banks.

2. Benefits and Risks of Public Debt

  • Why Borrowing Helps: It funds big projects like power plants, rail corridors, and highways that tax revenue alone cannot pay for. During slowdowns, it keeps public works going.
  • Key Risks to Watch:
    ▫ Heavy Interest Costs: Paying interest on old debt eats up a huge part of the annual budget, leaving less for health and schools.
    ▫ Crowding-Out Effect: When the government borrows heavily from banks, less money is left for private businesses, pushing up loan interest rates.
    ▫ Currency Exchange Risk: If the rupee weakens, foreign-currency loans become far more expensive to repay.
IV. Foreign Investment: FDI vs. FII Differences
Feature Foreign Direct Investment (FDI) Foreign Institutional Investment (FII / FPI)
Type of Investment Long-term money invested in physical companies, joint ventures, or building new factories in India. Long-lasting capital. Buying shares, stocks, or bonds in Indian financial markets to earn trading profits. Can enter and exit very quickly ("hot money").
Control over Business Gives foreign investors a direct say, voting power, and management control in the business. Investors have no active management control; they only hold paper shares for dividends and capital gains.
Impact on Real Economy Brings modern machinery, new management methods, global supply connections, and creates direct local jobs. Provides liquidity to stock markets, helps discover fair share prices, and increases domestic stock values.
Stability & Risk Cannot be pulled out overnight during panic; keeps balance of payments steady. Sudden mass selling by foreign funds can crash the rupee and cause big swings in the stock market.

FDI Trends & Sector-Wise Inflows

  • Gross FDI Inflow Milestone: India's gross Foreign Direct Investment reached a record high of $94.53 billion in 2025-26, rising 17% over the previous year.
  • Top Sectors Attracting FDI:
    ▫ Information Technology & Digital Services: 28.3%
    ▫ Healthcare & Hospitality: 18.1%
    ▫ Renewable Energy & Clean Tech: 15.5%
    ▫ Automobile & Electric Vehicle Supply Chain: 7.4%
    ▫ Specialty Chemicals & New Materials: 6.9%
    ▫ Construction, Urban Infrastructure & Logistics: 6.1%
    ▫ Telecom (5G & Satellite Services): 4.8%
    ▫ Drugs and Pharmaceuticals: 3.6%
V. Financial Markets & Developmental Finance Institutions

1. Main Parts of Indian Financial Markets

  • Capital Markets: Buy and sell long-term shares and company bonds. Main platforms: National Stock Exchange (NSE) and Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE).
  • Money Markets: Trade short-term loan papers (under 1 year) to manage daily bank and company cash flow:
    ▫ Treasury Bills (T-Bills): Short-term government debt papers.
    ▫ Commercial Papers (CPs): Short-term unsecured promissory notes issued by top companies.
    ▫ Certificates of Deposit (CDs): Short-term deposits issued by banks.
  • Derivatives Markets: Financial contracts (Futures and Options) whose values depend on an underlying share, currency, or commodity; used by businesses to protect against price swings.

2. Development Finance Institutions (DFIs)

  • What is a DFI: Specialized government institutions that lend patient, long-term money to high-risk, slow-return sectors where commercial banks hesitate to lend.
  • Major DFIs in India:
    ▫ NABARD: Focuses on farm loans, rural banks, irrigation projects, and village infrastructure.
    ▫ SIDBI: Provides venture capital, credit guarantees, and cheap loans to Small and Medium Enterprises (SMEs).
    ▫ IDFC & NaBFID: Lends long-term money to build power plants, roads, ports, and urban grids.
  • Core Role: Channels savings into national projects, supports neglected sectors, and reduces funding shortages.

Program Name Target, Ministry & Main Purpose Key Components & Ground Status

• Goal: Build seamless highway corridors to cut logistics costs from ~13–14% of GDP down to international levels of 8–10%.

• Status (2026): ~845 projects planned (~₹6.06 lakh cr); ~315 completed (~₹1.57 lakh cr). Sagarmala 2.0 expanding.

• Funding: Multi-tranche loan from Asian Development Bank (ADB).		 • Key Nodes: Visakhapatnam, Chittoor (Srikalahasti-Yerpedu), Kopparthy (Kadapa), Donakonda, Machilipatnam.
• Status (2026): Master plans notified; internal power, water, and road works underway in Kopparthy, Rambilli, and Naidupeta.
VII. Energy Resources & The 4-Stage Transition

Current Power Capacity (mid-2026)

  • Total Grid Capacity: ~549 GW installed.
  • Coal Still Dominates Generation: Coal is ~40–46% of total capacity, but it provides ~70% of actual power during summer peaks because solar and wind stop generating at night.
  • Renewable Capacity: ~288–290 GW installed:
    ▫ Solar Power: ~162 GW (largest clean source).
    ▫ Wind Power: ~57 GW.
    ▫ Large Hydro: ~57 GW.
  • Non-Fossil Share: Total clean capacity (Renewables + Nuclear ~8–9 GW) stands at ~297 GW, crossing 50% of total installed capacity.
  • Natural Gas: Produces less than 2–5% of power due to high imported LNG costs.

The 4-Stage Energy Transition Path

  • Stage 1: Coal as the Bridge: Will remain necessary till the mid-2030s for steady round-the-clock power while battery storage grows; uses coal gasification and efficient supercritical boilers.
  • Stage 2: Natural Gas for Flexible Balancing: Cleaner transition fuel used for city cooking gas (PNG), vehicles (CNG), and powering factories.
  • Stage 3: Fast-Tracking Renewables (Panchamrit Targets):
    ▫ Reach 500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030.
    ▫ Meet 50% of energy requirements from clean sources by 2030.
    ▫ Cut GDP emissions intensity by 45% by 2030.
  • Stage 4: Green Hydrogen (Net Zero by 2070): Zero-emission clean fuel to run heavy steel mills, fertilizer plants, and cargo trucks.

📌 Global Oil Storage Comparison:
• India's Actual Coverage: Government SPR caverns hold ~9.5 days of net imports; oil company tanks hold ~64.5 days $\rightarrow$ Total Country Storage = ~74 days.

IX. Flagship Clean Energy Programs & Schemes

1. National Green Hydrogen Mission (NGHM)

  • Started: January 2023 by MNRE; budget outlay of ₹19,744 crore till 2029–30.
  • 2030 Targets: Produce $\ge$ 5 Million Tonnes of green hydrogen per year; set up ~125 GW of clean power; bring > ₹8 lakh cr investments; create 6 lakh jobs.
  • SIGHT Program: Subsidies for manufacturing water electrolysers and producing green hydrogen.
  • Status: ~8,000 tonnes capacity running; contracts signed to supply green ammonia to fertilizer plants.

2. National Solar Mission (JNNSM)

  • Started: 2010 under National Climate Plan (original goal 20 GW; raised to 100 GW in 2015).
  • Status (June 2026): Installed solar capacity crossed 162.15 GW. India is the 3rd largest solar power producer globally.
  • Breakup: Large solar plants: ~125.6 GW; Rooftop solar: ~30.1 GW; Off-grid systems: ~6.4 GW.
  • Key Schemes: 56 sanctioned Solar Parks (~39,461 MW) and PLI scheme for domestic solar cells and panels.

3. National Biofuel Policy & E20 Petrol

  • Policy Updates: Amended in 2022 to allow using surplus maize, damaged rice grains, and farm waste to make ethanol.
  • E20 Milestone Achieved: The 20% ethanol blending target was moved forward from 2030 to 2025-26; achieved ahead of time in December 2025 / early 2026 across state petrol pumps.
  • Growth in Blending: Rose from 1.5% (2013-14) to 12% (2022-23), 19.2% (2024-25), and 20% in 2025-26.
  • Benefits: Saved > ₹1.9 lakh crore in foreign exchange; cut ~310+ lakh tonnes of crude oil imports; paid > ₹1.6 lakh crore to sugarcane and grain farmers.

4. PM-KUSUM, PM Surya Ghar & Offshore Wind

  • PM-KUSUM: Replaces diesel farm pumps with solar pumps. Component B: ~11.5 lakh standalone solar pumps installed. Component C: solarizing grid-connected irrigation pumps.
  • PM Surya Ghar (Feb 2024, ₹75,021 cr): Subsidies to install rooftop solar on 1 crore homes, giving up to 300 units of free power each month. Over 35 lakh homes already installed (~12–14 GW).
  • Offshore Wind: India has ~70 GW wind potential along Gujarat and Tamil Nadu coasts. Target of 30 GW by 2030 backed by ₹7,453 crore in viability gap funding.
X. Exam-Trick Boxes (Avoid Common Traps!)
  • TRAP 1: "Revenue expenditure creates new physical assets for the country." ➔ WRONG! Revenue spending does not create any assets; it covers daily running expenses like government salaries, pensions, subsidies, and interest payments. Capital expenditure is what builds new roads and assets.
  • TRAP 2: "FII investments bring long-term factory setups and managerial control." ➔ WRONG! FDI brings direct factory setups, new technology, and management control. FII only buys shares in financial markets without running company operations.
  • TRAP 3: "India's strategic oil reserves already meet the IEA 90-day requirement." ➔ WRONG! Government underground SPRs cover ~9.5 days; adding oil company commercial storage brings it to ~74 days, which is still below the IEA 90-day minimum.
  • TRAP 4: "Green Hydrogen is produced by splitting natural gas." ➔ WRONG! Splitting natural gas makes Grey or Blue hydrogen. Green hydrogen is made strictly by electrolysis of water powered by renewable electricity with zero carbon emissions.
  • TRAP 5: "The Visakhapatnam-Chennai Industrial Corridor (VCIC) is funded by the World Bank." ➔ WRONG! VCIC is funded through loans from the Asian Development Bank (ADB).
XI. Latest Current Affairs & Economic Developments (2025–2026)

1. Union Budget & Fiscal Consolidation

  • Fiscal Deficit Path: Central fiscal deficit targeted at 4.3% of GDP for 2026-27, down from 4.4% in 2025-26, maintaining the post-pandemic fiscal glide path.
  • High Public Capex: Central capital outlay maintained above ₹11 lakh crore, prioritizing National Highway completions, railway track doubling, and freight corridors.
  • National Monetization Gains: Cumulative monetization through toll-operate-transfer (TOT) highway concessions and InvITs crossed ₹1.5 lakh crore.

2. Foreign Investment & Clean Energy Tenders

  • Gross FDI Landmark: India logged an all-time record $94.53 billion in gross FDI during FY 2025-26, led by IT, digital services, and clean energy.
  • Insurance FDI Liberalization: Policy proposals tabled to increase the automatic FDI cap in insurance from 74% to 100%.
  • Green Hydrogen Tenders: SECI awarded capacity for ~3,000 MW domestic electrolyser manufacturing under SIGHT, along with green ammonia supply agreements with public refineries.

UPSC Prelims Practice • Question 1
Q1. Which of the following is/are classified as Capital Receipts in the Union Budget of India?
1. Market borrowings raised through Government Securities (G-Secs)
2. Disinvestment proceeds received from selling shares in Public Sector Enterprises
3. Dividend transfers received from the Reserve Bank of India
4. Recovery of loans extended to State Governments
Select the correct answer:
1) 1 and 2 only
2) 1, 2 and 4 only
3) 2, 3 and 4 only
4) 1, 2, 3 and 4
Show Answer & Catch
➔ Correct Answer: 2) 1, 2 and 4 only.
▫ Statements 1, 2, and 4 are Capital Receipts: Borrowings increase government liabilities, while disinvestment and recovering old loans reduce government assets.
▫ Statement 3 is a Revenue Receipt: Dividend profits received from the RBI or PSUs are non-tax Revenue Receipts because they do not reduce assets or create liabilities.

Mains Practice • 15 Marks Q2. "Analyze India’s 4-stage energy transition pathway (Coal $\rightarrow$ Natural Gas $\rightarrow$ Renewables $\rightarrow$ Green Hydrogen). How does this balance national energy security with international climate commitments?"
View Model Answer Structuring Framework
Answer Framework:
• Introduction: India's energy transition follows a step-by-step path to meet growing power needs while working toward the Panchamrit goals (500 GW clean capacity by 2030 and Net Zero by 2070).
• Four-Stage Transition Matrix:
Stage Role & Installed Status Strategy & Next Steps
1. Coal Dominance Produces ~70% of generation during peak summer; supplies steady round-the-clock power. Using efficient supercritical boilers and coal gasification; will remain a bridge fuel till the mid-2030s.
2. Natural Gas Cleaner transition fuel for vehicles (CNG) and home cooking (PNG). Produces <2–5% of power due to high imported LNG prices; useful for balancing evening peak demand.
3. Renewables Push Over 288 GW installed (Solar 162 GW, Wind 57 GW, Hydro 57 GW). Scaling rooftop solar through PM Surya Ghar (1 cr homes), PM-KUSUM for farmers, and grid battery storage (BESS).
4. Green Hydrogen Targeting 5 Million Tonnes per year by 2030. Decarbonizing heavy sectors like steel, refineries, and fertilizers using SIGHT production incentives.
• Protecting Energy Security: Expanding ethanol blending to 20% (E20), adding domestic solar panel manufacturing via PLI, and maintaining strategic underground crude oil reserves protect against international supply shocks.
• Conclusion: A phased, reliable transition ensures India has affordable round-the-clock power to expand factories while reducing carbon emissions over time.
